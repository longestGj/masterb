# The Ultimate Guide to Desiccant Masterbatch DM2476G

This guide answers practical selection, process, storage, and purchasing questions about GE CHEMICAL's 75.9% CaO desiccant masterbatch DM2476G for injection, extrusion, and film blowing.

### What Is Desiccant Masterbatch DM2476G?

DM2476G is a pelletized **desiccant masterbatch** from GE CHEMICAL that carries 75.9% grey calcium oxide (CaO) in a 24% recycled PE carrier, with no CaCO₃ filler. You dose the grey granules into wet or humidity-exposed resin so the CaO can absorb moisture during melting. The grade is built for general use in injection, extrusion, and film blowing, where steam-related defects are common and a low ~1% addition is the TDS starting point.

### What Is Desiccant Masterbatch?

A **desiccant masterbatch** (also called drying masterbatch or CaO masterbatch) is a functional concentrate whose job is moisture scavenging, not pigment or appearance. DM2476G carries an active dryer, calcium oxide, and is grey because of that CaO package.

### What Is the Difference Between Desiccant Masterbatch and Silica Gel Packets?

Silica gel packets sit in finished cartons or bags and protect packed goods after molding. **Desiccant masterbatch** works inside the melt during injection, extrusion, or film blowing. DM2476G is an in-process moisture absorber masterbatch; it does not replace carton desiccants for warehouse humidity after the product is made. Many plants use both: CaO masterbatch on the line, silica gel in export packs.

### How Does Calcium Oxide (CaO) Absorb Moisture in Plastic Processing?

Calcium oxide reacts with water to form calcium hydroxide, so free moisture that would otherwise flash to steam in the barrel can be chemically bound. That is why high CaO loading matters in a drying masterbatch. DM2476G lists 75.9% grey CaO, which means most of the pellet mass is dedicated to that scavenging function. The reaction is irreversible in normal processing terms — once CaO has taken up water, that capacity is spent — which is why vacuum packaging and dry storage matter so much for this grade.

### Why Is DM2476G Grey?

The TDS lists Appearance as Grey Granule and CaO as Grey CaO at 75.9%. The grey tone comes from the active desiccant package, not from carbon black pigment. Do not treat DM2476G as a substitute for [black masterbatch](/black-masterbatch) or [white masterbatch](/white-masterbatch). If your part must stay a clean natural or bright white shade, trial the dose carefully, because a grey CaO concentrate can shift color at higher addition rates.

### What Does 75.9% CaO Content Mean Commercially?

It means nearly three-quarters of each kilogram of pellet is grey calcium oxide. Higher CaO content usually means stronger drying capacity per kilogram of concentrate, which supports the low recommended dosage of about 1%. Loading alone does not replace a line trial, but it is the first commercial lever when you compare a high-CaO grade like DM2476G with lower-activity drying pellets that need heavier dosing.

### Why Does the TDS Show 0% CaCO₃ Filler?

DM2476G contains no CaCO₃ filler. The pellet is CaO plus recycled PE carrier, not a limestone-padded bag. That design keeps mass in the active dryer and the polymer bridge. Filled color grades often use CaCO₃ for cost; a desiccant grade is judged by drying power, so zero filler on this TDS is a deliberate product choice rather than a missing line item.

### What Carrier Resin Does DM2476G Use?

The carrier is 24% recycled PE. The carrier is the polymer bridge that melts into polyethylene-compatible streams and carries the CaO with it. Because the share is modest versus the 75.9% CaO, most of the pellet is functional desiccant. Recycled PE carrier is stated openly on the TDS — do not hide it in purchasing talks. Confirm that your process and any customer recycled-content rules accept a recycled PE carrier before you lock the grade.

### What Are the Processing Implications of a Recycled PE Carrier?

Recycled PE can vary more than virgin PE in melt behavior and residual contamination, depending on feedstock quality. For many general PE injection, extrusion, and film jobs, a recycled PE carrier still mixes acceptably when dosage is only about 1%. For critical optical film, food-contact programs, or buyer specs that ban recycled content in additives, tell us before you standardize DM2476G. We will not invent a virgin-carrier claim that the TDS does not support.

### Is DM2476G Suitable for Food-Contact Applications?

The TDS for DM2476G does not list a food-grade flag or named food-contact regulations. Do not assume FDA, EU, or other food-contact clearance for this grade. If your project requires food-contact documentation, ask for SDS and any declarations we can actually supply, or select a different additive path that matches your compliance file. Honest qualification beats marketing language that outruns the sheet.

### What Is the Recommended Dosage for DM2476G?

The TDS recommends about 1% addition. That low dose is possible because CaO content is high at 75.9%. Start near 1% on a short trial, then adjust based on bubble count, splay, surface finish, and any color shift from the grey pellet. Too little leaves moisture defects; too much wastes concentrate and can push grey tint or mechanical side effects. Lock the ratio only after your own machine proves the result.

### How Should I Run a First Trial of Desiccant Masterbatch at 1%?

Confirm resin type, whether regrind is in the mix, hopper and bag storage history, process temperatures, and the defect you want to cut (bubbles, splay, gels, voids). Dose DM2476G near 1%, keep packaging sealed until use, and compare against a control lot without desiccant under the same conditions. Keep retained samples of both the masterbatch and finished parts. A plaque test helps; a real injection, extrusion, or film line is the decisive check.

### Can I Use DM2476G for Injection Molding?

Yes. The TDS lists injection among the general-use applications. In injection molding, moisture often shows up as splay marks, silver streaks, bubbles, or weak weld areas. A **desiccant masterbatch for injection molding** like DM2476G helps when resin or regrind carries water into the barrel. It is not a cure for wrong melt temperature, short shot, or poor venting — diagnose those separately while you trial the ~1% dose.

### Can I Use DM2476G for Film Blowing?

Yes. Film blowing is listed on the TDS. Thin film makes moisture defects very visible as bubbles, fish-eyes, gels, or unstable bubble behavior. A **desiccant masterbatch for film blowing** is useful when flake, outdoor-stored resin, or humid plant air loads moisture into the melt. Keep addition near the recommended 1% and watch gauge, haze, and any grey tint, because film optics amplify small color shifts.

### Can I Use DM2476G for Extrusion?

Yes. Extrusion is listed alongside injection and film blowing. Pipe, sheet, profile, and compounding lines all suffer when wet feedstock flashes in the screw. A **desiccant masterbatch for extrusion** helps scavenge that moisture during melting. Match expectations to your product: structural extrusion may tolerate a slight grey cast better than clear sheet. Trial first, then standardize.

### When Should I Add Desiccant Masterbatch Instead of Only Pre-Drying Resin?

Pre-drying and dry storage remain first-line controls. Use DM2476G when moisture still reaches the process — for example washed regrind that never fully dries, resin stored outdoors, open hoppers in humid seasons, or plants without a dedicated dryer for every stream. Desiccant masterbatch is a processing safety net, not a license to ignore wet bags. The best plants combine dry handling with a measured CaO dose when risk is high.

### How Does Moisture Cause Bubbles, Splay, and Gels in Plastic Parts?

Water turns to steam under melt temperatures. Steam expands into bubbles and voids, races along flow fronts as splay, and can leave gels or surface roughness when local moisture and contamination interact. Thin film and glossy molded faces show these defects first. DM2476G targets the moisture itself by binding water with CaO; it does not remove dirt gels that come from contamination alone.

### Will Desiccant Masterbatch Fix Every Bubble Problem?

No. Bubbles can also come from trapped air, decomposition gases, wrong temperature, or poor venting. If your defects continue after a controlled 1% DM2476G trial with dry packaging and known resin history, look beyond moisture. Use the desiccant grade as a diagnostic lever: if bubbles drop sharply, moisture was a major cause; if nothing changes, hunt mechanical or thermal causes next.

Store cartons indoors, off the floor, away from rain, steam, and open water sources. Keep vacuum bags sealed until use. Ambient humidity can partially activate the CaO before processing, reducing the drying power left for your melt. You may then dose "1%" and still see bubbles because part of the capacity was spent in the warehouse. Treat opened vacuum bags as time-sensitive. If a bag was left open in humid air, run a cautious trial or replace it rather than assuming full TDS performance.

### Is CaO Masterbatch Hazardous to Handle, and What Documents Should I Request?

Calcium oxide reacts with water and can be irritating; treat it as a reactive industrial chemical, not as inert filler. We do not invent SDS hazard codes or GHS phrases on this marketing page. ### How Does DM2476G Compare With Simply Raising Process Temperature to "Drive Off" Moisture?

Higher temperature can sometimes reduce visible moisture marks, but it can also degrade polymer, raise gels, and waste energy without chemically binding water. A CaO desiccant binds moisture in the melt at a controlled ~1% dose. Many plants find that a measured drying masterbatch is more predictable than chasing humidity with temperature alone — especially for regrind-heavy recipes.
