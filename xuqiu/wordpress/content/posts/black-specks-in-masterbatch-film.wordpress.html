Black specks, dark streaks and uneven color in plastic film can come from several parts of the process. The visible defect identifies where to begin looking. It does not identify the cause.

A batch of masterbatch may be involved. So might the base resin, feeding setup, machine condition, purge procedure, screen pack, temperature profile, contamination source or observation method. Starting with one cause before collecting a comparison sample often turns a solvable quality issue into an argument.

This guide gives buyers, processors and suppliers one sequence for collecting useful evidence and deciding the next test.

Describe the defect before discussing responsibility

Use observable language. "Black specks about 0.3 mm wide, concentrated near the bubble edge, present continuously in the current run" gives more information than "the masterbatch is bad."

Record:

  • Defect type: speck, gel, particle, streak, color patch or other visible pattern
  • Size and approximate frequency
  • Position in the film, across the bubble or in a particular layer
  • Whether the defect appears on the surface, inside the film or throughout the structure
  • When it started and which run or batch was involved
  • Whether it appears continuously, intermittently or only at start-up
  • Whether the film thickness or machine settings changed
  • Which products and batches were present when the defect appeared

A photograph helps, but it should not be the only evidence. Include the full film or roll in at least one view, then take closer views with a scale or reference object. Record lighting and camera distance so another person can interpret the image.

Secure a control sample

A control sample gives the defect a comparison point. It should come from a run that met the agreed quality requirement and should have enough production information to make the comparison meaningful.

Record the control sample's:

  • Masterbatch grade and batch
  • Base resin grade and batch
  • Film structure and thickness
  • Addition ratio and feeding method
  • Machine and main processing conditions
  • Production date
  • Inspection result or acceptance record

If no control sample exists, collect the closest available good production sample and state what is unknown. A comparison with an unidentified sample can still help, but it should not be treated as a controlled test.

Collect the evidence pack

A useful first evidence pack contains six groups.

Keep the problem and control samples labelled. A sample without a product reference, batch, date and source becomes difficult to use after a few days.

Check five possible directions

The evidence pack supports a structured review. It does not create a hierarchy of blame.

Material

Check whether the masterbatch, resin or another raw material changed. Record batch references, storage conditions, drying records and any substitution or re-sourcing.

Ask for the TDS for the grade in use and the COA or inspection record for the affected batch where available. A current document describes or reports a defined product scope. It does not, by itself, explain a visible defect.

Addition and feeding

Confirm the addition ratio that was planned and the ratio actually delivered to the melt. Review calibration, feeder stability, pre-mixing, separate feeding, reclaim addition and refill practice.

A change in apparent color or dispersion may follow a change in effective addition. The feeding records should be compared with the control run rather than assumed.

Process

Compare temperature, output, speed, pressure, bubble stability and filtration records. Look for changes around the first appearance of the defect.

A single temperature reading may not describe the heat history of the material. Barrel residence time, shutdowns, low-output periods and start-up conditions can also matter. Record what is available.

Equipment

Review the screw, barrel, die, screen pack and material path. Check maintenance, purge, color-change and cleaning records. A machine that is mechanically different from the control run may produce a different visible result.

Filtration evidence should be treated as one clue. A pressure rise or screen blockage shows that material is being captured. It does not establish what the material is or where it entered the process.

Environment and handling

Check material storage, open containers, dust controls, cleaning, regrind handling and handover between shifts. A contamination event may leave no record in the masterbatch batch file.

Use a comparison sequence

1. Confirm that the defect is repeatable

Inspect more than one section of the affected run. A single speck can be a local event. A pattern that repeats under the same conditions provides a stronger basis for a controlled test.

2. Compare with the control run

Place the problem film and control film side by side. Use the same lighting and observation distance. Look for differences in speck frequency, streak direction, color and position through the film.

An empty cell is useful. It identifies the evidence that still needs to be collected.

4. Change one variable at a time

Where production allows, run a controlled comparison with the same resin, machine, addition ratio and conditions. Change one recorded variable, such as the masterbatch batch, then compare the result.

If several variables change at once, the test may reveal an improvement or deterioration, but it will not identify which change produced the result.

5. Record the result

Record:

  • Materials and batch references
  • Machine and conditions
  • Change introduced
  • Samples collected
  • Observation or test result
  • Person who approved the conclusion

The conclusion should stay within the test scope. A result from one line, product and batch does not automatically cover another.

Separate defect types

Different visible patterns need different records. The names below describe appearance. They do not establish a source.

Black specks

Record size, count, shape, distribution and whether the speck sits on the surface or inside the film. Retain the problem film and the raw materials used in the run.

Dark streaks

Record direction, width, location across the bubble and whether the streak continues through the roll. Note layer position in a co-extruded film and any die or flow-path work.

Gels or unmelted particles

Record shape and whether the particle deforms under heat or pressure. Compare with the control film and inspect the resin and masterbatch records. A visual particle identification may need laboratory support.

Uneven color

Record the color reference, measured result where available, addition ratio, resin and film thickness. Do not assign the difference to dispersion before checking the comparison conditions.

Surface deposits or die build-up

Record where the deposit forms, when it starts and what material remains after cleaning. Include purge and shutdown records because the material history may differ from steady-state production.

Customer complaint intake

When a customer reports a film defect, the first reply should obtain enough evidence to schedule the right review.

Ask in phases. A first message can request the problem sample, control sample, product references and approximate production date. Detailed machine records can follow once the samples show that a technical review is needed.

What a supplier response should show

A useful supplier response identifies what is known, what is missing and what can be tested. It does not immediately name a cause or deny responsibility.

A weak response:

Our masterbatch cannot cause black specks. The problem is in your machine.

A more useful response:

We need the affected film, a control film, the masterbatch and resin batch references, the addition ratio and the main process records. The cause is not yet confirmed. We can compare the masterbatch batch against the retained sample and arrange a controlled trial after reviewing the feeding and screen-pack history.

The second response creates a path to evidence. It still leaves the cause open.

A first technical reply template

We have received the report of [defect] from [production date or roll reference]. At this stage the cause is not confirmed.

To review it, please provide:

• Problem film and a control film
• Masterbatch and resin grades and batch references
• Film structure and thickness
• Addition ratio and feeder records
• Main temperature, output and pressure records
• Screen-pack, purge and maintenance history
• Photographs showing the defect and its position

After the samples and records are reviewed, we will propose the next comparison or trial. The scope of that trial will be agreed before it starts.

Prevent the next unclear investigation

Most complaint delays begin before the defect. Production records are incomplete, samples are not labelled, or the masterbatch batch cannot be linked to the roll.

The following controls make future reviews easier:

  • Record masterbatch and resin batches at start-up and at material changes
  • Keep a retention sample for each masterbatch lot as agreed with the supplier
  • Record the actual addition ratio and feeder calibration
  • Keep start-up, purge and shutdown records
  • Label problem samples with machine, product, date and roll
  • Keep a control sample from an approved run
  • Record process changes against the production date
  • Define who will approve a return to normal production

These controls do not prevent every defect. They make the cause easier to test when a defect appears.
