The carbon black percentage on a masterbatch TDS is useful, but it does not choose the grade for you. A 40% product can deliver more pigment per kilogram than a 20% product. It can also cost more, feed differently, require a different let-down ratio and change the surface of the finished part.

The right starting point is the final product and the process that will make it. Buyers then compare the carbon black content with the carrier, filler, dispersion, target blackness and cost in use. This guide explains that comparison and the evidence needed before a grade is approved for production.

What carbon black content in black masterbatch means

Carbon black content is the share of carbon black in the masterbatch pellet. It is not the carbon black content of the finished plastic part. The let-down ratio determines how much masterbatch enters the final formulation.

For example, 4 kg of a 30% grade added to 100 kg of compound contains about 1.2 kg of carbon black, before losses. The same addition rate with a 40% grade contains about 1.6 kg. The higher-loading grade can supply more pigment at the same feed setting, but that arithmetic does not yet prove that it will disperse well or meet the appearance target.

The percentage also does not identify the carbon black itself. Carbon black grades can differ in particle size, structure and surface treatment. Those differences can affect tone, color development and the amount of work needed to disperse the pigment. Two products with the same headline percentage can therefore behave differently in the same resin and process.

Read carbon black, carrier and filler as one system

A black masterbatch contains more than carbon black. The current TDS examples for BK020 to BK040 list carbon black, an LLDPE and LDPE carrier, a dispersion agent and CaCO3 filler. Their shares change together.

These values belong to the listed TDS examples. Ask for the current TDS for the grade being offered and check that the grade, revision and values have not changed.

Across this range, carbon black rises from 20% to 40% while the listed CaCO3 filler falls from 47.8% to 5.8%. A lower-loading product can therefore contain much more filler. A higher-loading product can deliver more carbon black and less filler per kilogram, subject to its actual formulation.

The carrier is the second part of the comparison. The current BK examples use the same resin family, but the carrier share is not the same in every grade. A buyer still needs to confirm the carrier against the actual base resin, processing temperature and final product. A familiar abbreviation such as PE or PP is not enough to approve compatibility.

How the 20% to 40% range changes

Loading bands are a way to narrow the trial, not a universal grade chart. A 20% grade from one supplier is not interchangeable with a 20% grade from another supplier if the carbon black, carrier, filler or dispersion changes.

Lower loadings around 20% to 25%

Lower-loading grades leave more room for filler in the examples above. They can be a practical starting direction when the final product is thick enough to carry more filler and the appearance target is less demanding. Trash bags, general molded parts and re-granulating are application directions listed in the current BK TDS examples.

The lower pigment share normally requires more masterbatch to deliver a target amount of carbon black. Buyers should check whether the lower compound price offsets the higher use rate. They should also check gloss, mechanical requirements and surface appearance if filler content matters to the product.

The middle of the range around 30%

A 30% grade can offer a useful balance between pigment load, carrier, filler and feed rate. It is often a reasonable grade to test when the application is known but the final loading has not been fixed.

That does not make 30% a default answer for every product. A thin film may need a different dispersion or surface result. A high-gloss molded part may need a lower filler level. A cost-sensitive thick section may work with less carbon black and more filler. The trial still decides.

Higher loadings around 35% to 40%

Higher-loading grades can deliver more pigment with less masterbatch. The BK040 example contains 40% carbon black and 5.8% CaCO3 filler. That combination can be relevant when the buyer needs a deep black, a cleaner high-gloss surface or a lower feed rate.

The trade-off is not limited to price per kilogram. A concentrated grade still has to feed consistently, melt with the base resin and disperse at the final addition ratio. A high percentage that creates specks, streaks or feeding variation has not solved the buyer's problem.

Why blackness alone does not settle the grade

Blackness describes the result the buyer wants to see or measure. It does not describe the full formulation that produces that result. The final appearance depends on the carbon black, pigment load, dispersion, carrier, filler, part thickness, surface finish and viewing or measurement method.

Thin film makes dispersion defects easy to see. An undispersed particle can appear as a speck or streak even when the TDS lists a high carbon black content. A molded part may hide small specks but make surface haze, flow lines or gloss differences more obvious.

Define blackness with a physical reference where possible. A retained sample, an approved production part or a measured color target is more useful than a description such as "very black". State the resin, masterbatch addition ratio, sample preparation and measurement conditions used for a numeric color result. A color number without those details is difficult to compare.

Calculate cost from the use rate

Price per kilogram is only one input. The useful comparison is the material cost required to make a defined quantity of acceptable product.

Masterbatch price per kg x let-down ratio x 1,000 = material cost per tonne

Use 0.05 for a 5% let-down ratio and 0.03 for a 3% ratio.

For illustration only, a USD 4.00 grade used at 5% gives USD 200 of masterbatch per tonne. A USD 6.00 grade used at 3% gives USD 180 per tonne. The higher-priced product may cost less to use, but only if the lower feed rate reaches the same appearance and runs reliably on the line.

The current BK TDS examples list a recommended addition ratio of 2% to 5%, with adjustment for actual production conditions. Treat that as a starting recommendation tied to the listed products, not a guarantee that every grade will meet the buyer's target inside that range.

Cost in use should also include the effects of failed parts, rejected film, line stops, feeding variation, purge time and any change in mechanical or surface properties. A quotation cannot settle those effects. A controlled trial and production record can.

Match the starting grade to the product and process

The following are trial directions, not automatic grade approvals:

  • Trash bags and general thick sections can begin with a lower or middle loading where filler is acceptable and the blackness target allows it.
  • Blown film should start with dispersion and surface quality. Test the exact resin, film structure, thickness and final let-down ratio. A higher-loading grade may reduce feed demand, but it still needs to run cleanly.
  • Injection molding may place more emphasis on color consistency, surface finish and flow. Compare the trial parts under the same lighting and measurement method.
  • Re-granulating and recycled feedstock can contain color variation. Black can mask some of that variation, but the required addition rate will depend on the feedstock and target appearance.

Check the documents against the exact grade

A TDS is the first document for grade-specific values. Check the product name, revision, carbon black content, carrier, filler and recommended addition range. Ask which values are typical and which, if any, are acceptance limits.

An SDS covers handling, storage and transport information for the product and version stated in that document. A COA covers a specific production batch. A compliance report or declaration covers the exact grade, sample, standard and test named in it.

A statement such as "food grade" in a TDS does not by itself prove that the exact grade may be used in a food-contact application in the buyer's market. Ask for a current document that covers the grade, application, test method and regulatory scope required for the destination market.

Use the technical document request process when the required TDS, SDS, COA or compliance scope has not been confirmed for the exact grade.

Prepare a useful inquiry

An inquiry that says only "best price for black masterbatch" leaves every supplier to guess. Include:

  • Base resin and grade, if known
  • Process: blown film, injection molding, extrusion or another method
  • Final product, wall thickness or film structure where relevant
  • Appearance target and a physical or measured color reference
  • Current grade and addition ratio, if replacing an existing material
  • Trial quantity and expected monthly volume
  • Required documents and target market
  • Known processing limits or current production problem

The masterbatch RFQ checklist provides a consistent input list. A complete brief lets the supplier recommend a carbon black range without pretending that one grade fits every line.

Run a comparable trial

Keep the trial controlled. Record the base resin, masterbatch grade and batch, addition ratio, feeder setting, machine, temperature profile, output rate, film thickness or part dimensions, and inspection method.

Keep a control sample made under the same conditions without the candidate change, or use a recent approved production sample with enough records to make the comparison useful. Compare blackness, dispersion, surface, feeding stability and any mechanical requirement that matters to the product.

Run more than one addition ratio when the supplier's starting recommendation is wide. The result should show the lowest ratio that meets the agreed requirements and remains stable through a realistic production run. A single hand sample may show that a grade can make a black part. It does not prove that the grade is ready for an order.

Choose the grade after the evidence

There is no universal best carbon black content. A lower-loading grade may lower material cost when filler and appearance are acceptable. A higher-loading grade may reduce feed demand and support a cleaner surface when the line can disperse and feed it consistently.

Choose the grade that meets the product target at a stable addition ratio, with the carrier and documents reviewed for the actual application. Compare cost per tonne after the trial, not before it.
