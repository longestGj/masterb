A black injection-molded part can fail visually before it fails mechanically. Specks, streaks, gloss variation and weld-line read-through are easy to see under plant lighting, and they often appear after a grade has already been quoted.

The visible defect does not identify the cause. The masterbatch, base resin, drying, feeding, screw, mold, gate and process all contribute. This guide sets out the information a buyer should collect before choosing a grade, the grade comparisons that matter for injection molding, and the trial conditions that make a result comparable.

Start with the molded part, not the carbon black percentage

A masterbatch TDS describes a concentrate. It does not describe the part, the tool or the machine. Before comparing grades, record five inputs that change the shortlist:

  • Base resin and grade, including MFI where known.
  • Wall thickness, flow length and the position of the gate.
  • Surface target: high gloss, matte, textured or a visible cosmetic face.
  • Weld-line locations and any strength or appearance requirement around them.
  • End use, target market and the documents the buyer needs to review.

These inputs matter because the same grade can behave differently in a thin cosmetic wall and a thick, hidden section. A long flow path gives the melt more time to cool and more opportunity for an undispersed particle to reach the surface. A high-gloss tool shows specks that a textured surface would hide.

The masterbatch RFQ checklist can be used to collect these inputs in one format before contacting a supplier.

What changes across the 20% to 40% carbon black range

The current BK020 to BK040 TDS examples list carbon black, an LLDPE and LDPE carrier, a dispersion agent and CaCO3 filler. The shares change together, and the filler difference is large enough to affect surface, density and cost in use.

These values belong to the listed TDS examples. Ask for the current TDS for the grade being offered and check the grade, revision and values before using the table for a decision.

The current BK product pages also list 0.2% maximum moisture and a 2% to 5% recommended addition range, with adjustment for actual production conditions. The current BK020 to BK040 pages list an adjustable MFI of 3 to 50 g/10 min at 190°C and 21.6 kg. Confirm the exact grade and revision, because a page summary is not a substitute for the current grade-specific TDS.

Lower loadings around 20% to 25%

Lower-loading grades contain more filler in the current examples. They can be a practical direction for thick sections, cost-sensitive parts or applications where the appearance target is less demanding. The trade-off is a higher use rate to deliver a target amount of carbon black, plus the effect of the filler on gloss, density and mechanical behavior.

The middle of the range around 30%

A 30% grade is often a reasonable starting point when the part is known but the final loading has not been fixed. It offers a middle position between pigment load, carrier, filler and feed rate. It is not a default answer: a high-gloss cosmetic part and a thick hidden part can still need different directions.

Higher loadings around 35% to 40%

Higher-loading grades can deliver more carbon black with less masterbatch and less filler per kilogram. That can support a deeper black, a cleaner high-gloss surface or a lower feed rate, provided the line can feed and disperse the concentrate consistently. A high percentage that produces specks or flow instability has not solved the appearance problem.

The carbon black content guide compares the same range from a cost-in-use and trial perspective. Use it for the broader loading comparison; use this guide for the injection-specific checks.

Dispersion is the injection-specific risk

Injection molding applies high shear for a short time. A carbon black agglomerate that survives that history can appear as a speck on a cosmetic face, a dark streak along the flow direction or a roughness that only shows after polishing or assembly.

Dispersion is not the same as carbon black percentage. A 40% grade can be well dispersed in one setup and poorly dispersed in another if the carrier, MFI, let-down ratio, feeder or temperature profile do not match the resin and the tool. The percentage tells the buyer how much pigment is present, not how much work the material can take before the pigment is evenly distributed.

  • Compare the problem part with a control part from an approved run.
  • Record the masterbatch grade, batch, resin grade and batch, and the actual let-down ratio.
  • Check the feeder calibration, purge procedure and any material or color change.
  • Review the temperature profile, back pressure, screw speed and residence time.
  • Inspect the screen pack and pressure history without treating a pressure rise as proof of the cause.

If the defect appears after a batch or material change, the black specks diagnostic guide sets out a comparison sequence that also applies to molded parts. The goal is evidence, not an immediate assignment of blame.

Match MFI, carrier and let-down to the machine

MFI describes how a material flows under a stated temperature and load. It is useful for comparing products measured under the same conditions, but it does not predict the pressure, shear or cooling inside a specific mold. The carrier has to melt and mix within the process window of the base resin, and the let-down ratio has to deliver enough pigment without overloading the feed or the mixing section.

A PE carrier is a starting direction for a PE part, not proof that every PE grade or every tool has been checked. PP, engineering resins and filled compounds need their own review. When the resin changes, ask which part of the previous trial remains relevant and which part has to be repeated.

The current BK TDS examples list a 2% to 5% recommended addition range. Treat that as a starting recommendation for the listed grades, not a universal dosage for every part. The actual ratio should be confirmed at the target wall thickness, surface and cycle time, with the feeding method and back pressure recorded.

Filler, gloss and mechanical trade-offs

CaCO3 filler is not only a cost component. In the current examples it changes from 47.8% in BK020 to 5.8% in BK040. That difference can affect surface gloss, density, shrinkage behavior and the way the part reads under light. A buyer comparing price per kilogram without the use rate and the filler level is comparing different products.

Where a no-filler direction is required, PT-450P is the current 42% P-type grade with a 57.8% virgin LLDPE carrier and no filler in its TDS. Its TDS also carries a Food Grade label. That label is not a market-specific food-contact approval and should not be extended to other grades or applications without the corresponding documents.

Mechanical properties, impact behavior and long-term performance depend on the part design, resin, filler, dispersion and process. A masterbatch TDS can support a trial direction; it cannot prove the performance of a molded part. Ask for part-level test data where the application requires it.

Run an injection trial that can be compared

A trial is useful only when both sides know what is being compared. Agree the material, the part, the machine, the process window and the decision rule before the sample arrives.

Keep the problem part and the control part under the same lighting and at the same viewing distance. A photograph is useful evidence, but it should not replace the physical samples and the machine records.

One accepted trial supports one material in one part, tool and process window. It does not prove every future batch, another machine or another mold. Ask separately how the supplier controls batch consistency and which records accompany production.

Confirm the documents by exact grade

A TDS identifies the grade and lists typical or specified properties. A COA covers a specific production batch. An SDS covers handling, storage and transport for the product and version it names. A compliance report or declaration covers only the grade, sample, standard and test shown in it.

Check the document version, the grade and the test scope before relying on any of them. A Food Grade label, a RoHS report for one sample or a statement written for one market does not establish that every black grade is suitable for every application. Where the document scope is unclear, use the technical document request process and keep the request tied to the exact grade, application and destination market.

Next step: share the part and application

Send the team the information that changes the grade decision: base resin and grade, part and wall thickness, flow length and gate position, surface target, current grade or problem, trial quantity or monthly volume, and the documents required for the target market. With those inputs, a grade direction and a trial plan can be reviewed instead of guessed from a headline percentage.
