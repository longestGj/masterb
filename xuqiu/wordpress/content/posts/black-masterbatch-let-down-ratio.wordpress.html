A buyer with a 40% carbon black masterbatch and a target of 2.5% carbon black in the final compound can calculate a starting addition rate in one step. The arithmetic is simple. The risk is treating that number as the final dosage before the resin, feeder, film or part and trial conditions have been checked.

This guide explains what the let-down ratio measures, how to calculate it from the carbon black content of the masterbatch, how to convert the result into a feeder rate, and what to verify on the line. The examples are calculation examples, not recommended dosages for a particular product.

What the let-down ratio actually measures

The let-down ratio is the amount of masterbatch added to the final compound, usually expressed as a percentage by weight. If 5 kg of masterbatch is added to 95 kg of resin to make 100 kg of compound, the addition rate is 5% by weight of the final compound.

That is not the same as the carbon black content of the masterbatch. A grade listed at 40% carbon black contains 40% carbon black in the concentrate, together with a carrier and any filler. It does not put 40% carbon black into the final film or molded part.

Confirm the basis: final compound or resin

Percentage addition is normally stated on the final compound, but ratio notation can be ambiguous. A supplier may describe a 5% addition as 1:20, while another may use 1:20 to mean one part masterbatch to twenty parts resin. The first is 5% of the total; the second is about 4.76% of the total. Confirm the basis before converting a verbal ratio into a feeder setting.

This guide uses percentage by weight of the final compound throughout. That keeps the calculation consistent with the feeder check and the trial record.

The calculation formula

When the target is a defined carbon black content, the starting addition rate can be calculated from the carbon black content of the masterbatch:

Masterbatch addition % = target carbon black % / carbon black content in masterbatch % × 100

For example, a target of 2.5% carbon black with a 40% carbon black masterbatch gives 2.5 / 40 × 100 = 6.25%. The result is the starting masterbatch addition rate by weight, before line and trial checks.

The formula assumes both percentages use the same basis and that the target is expressed as carbon black content. It does not convert a blackness target, an opacity target or a visual benchmark into a carbon black percentage. Where the target is appearance rather than a defined carbon black content, the formula can only organize a starting point; the grade still has to be confirmed in a trial.

Why the calculated ratio is only a starting point

A calculated addition rate assumes that the target carbon black content is the only variable. In production, several other variables change the result.

Carrier and MFI compatibility

The carrier must be compatible with the base resin and the process. A grade that feeds well in one resin or on one machine may behave differently in another. Check the current TDS for the carrier and any MFI information, and confirm the grade against the actual resin and process rather than the carbon black percentage alone.

Filler and density

The current BK examples include CaCO3 filler, and the filler share changes across the range. Filler affects density, cost, surface and the meaning of a weight-based addition rate. Two masterbatches with the same carbon black content can therefore need different handling even before the line trial.

Dispersion and filtration

A correct addition rate does not guarantee dispersion. Poorly dispersed carbon black can appear as specks, streaks or gels, and a rising screen pack pressure can have more than one cause. The black specks diagnostic guide sets out a comparison sequence for film defects.

Process, thickness and equipment

Film thickness, part wall thickness, screw design, feeder accuracy and output all change the practical result. The current BK TDS examples list a 2% to 5% recommended addition range with adjustment for actual production conditions. That range is a starting point for the listed grades, not a fixed answer for every film, part or machine.

Set and verify the feeder rate

The calculated percentage has to become a feeder setting. For a weight-based feeder, the required masterbatch mass per hour is:

Masterbatch kg/h = compound output kg/h × masterbatch addition %

For example, a line running at 500 kg/h with a calculated addition rate of 6.25% requires 500 × 0.0625 = 31.25 kg/h of masterbatch. The example is illustrative; the actual output and ratio must come from the line and the agreed trial conditions.

Calibrate the feeder with a weighed sample or a timed output check. Record the set point, the actual output and the screw or line speed. Do not rely on the feeder display alone, because feeder calibration, refill behavior and screw speed can change the delivered rate.

Also confirm whether the feeder calculation is based on total compound or resin. A percentage that looks correct on paper can be applied to the wrong basis and produce a different final carbon black content.

What to check in the trial

The calculated rate should be tested against a control sample made with the same resin, structure or part, thickness and machine conditions. Keep the comparison narrow enough that a difference can be attributed to the masterbatch or the ratio, not to several changes at once.

  • Dispersion and specks: inspect the film or part under the agreed lighting and viewing distance.
  • Blackness and opacity: compare at the same thickness and with the same reference sample.
  • Screen pack pressure: record the starting pressure, the pressure rise and the time or output over which it occurred.
  • Surface: check gloss, streaks, gels, flow marks and any cosmetic requirement.
  • Output and feeder stability: confirm that the feeder holds the set rate through refills and normal line changes.
  • Batch record: keep the masterbatch grade, batch, actual addition rate and trial conditions with the samples.

One accepted trial supports one material in one resin, structure, thickness and process window. It does not prove every future batch, another machine or another product. Ask separately how the supplier controls batch consistency and which records accompany production.

Compare cost at the same carbon black target

Comparing masterbatch prices without adjusting for the addition rate gives a misleading result. The cost of masterbatch per kilogram of final compound is:

Masterbatch cost per kg compound = masterbatch price per kg × masterbatch addition rate

For example, a 40% grade at 6.25% addition and a 30% grade at 8.3% addition can be compared on the same target carbon black content. The grade with the lower price per kilogram is not necessarily the lower-cost option once the addition rate, filler, density and trial result are included. This article does not quote prices or promise a cost saving; it only sets out the comparison method.

Use the same target, the same units and the same cost basis for both grades. If one quote includes packaging, freight or a different quantity basis, adjust the comparison before drawing a conclusion.

What to send with an RFQ

A useful inquiry gives the supplier enough information to check the grade instead of guessing from a percentage. Include:

  • Target carbon black content, or the appearance or opacity target if carbon black content is not defined.
  • Current grade and actual addition rate, if one is already in use.
  • Base resin, process, film structure or part thickness, and any filler already in the compound.
  • Machine, output and feeder type, including the basis used for the addition rate.
  • Trial quantity, expected monthly quantity and destination market.
  • TDS, COA, SDS or other document needs, tied to the exact grade and application.

With those inputs, the team can review a grade direction and a trial plan. The calculation narrows the starting point; the line trial decides whether the ratio and the grade are usable.
