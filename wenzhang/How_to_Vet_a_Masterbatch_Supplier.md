Most supplier comparisons begin with a price. That is too early. Before comparing prices, a buyer needs to establish what each supplier is actually offering, which legal entity is responsible, what the technical documents cover, and whether the quoted grade can be checked on the buyer's own resin and process.

A useful supplier review does not prove that every future batch will perform in the same way. It shows whether the supplier can answer specific questions, provide traceable records, and define the limits of its own claims. That is the standard this checklist uses.

## Define the purchase before you judge the supplier

A supplier cannot give a comparable answer if the inquiry only says "need black masterbatch" or "send your best price". Prepare the same product brief for every supplier:

- Base resin and grade, if known
- Processing method and the most relevant operating conditions
- Final product, structure, dimensions or wall thickness where relevant
- Color, appearance or functional target
- Current problem, if one exists
- Current grade or supplier, if the material is being replaced
- Trial quantity and expected monthly volume
- Target market and required documents
- Packaging, destination and required delivery date, if already known

The [masterbatch RFQ checklist](/resources/masterbatch-rfq-checklist/) explains these inputs in more detail. Sending a consistent brief makes supplier replies easier to compare and makes vague answers easier to spot.

## Verify who you are buying from

The company on the website is not always the company that will issue the quotation, invoice or technical document. Ask for the legal entity name, registered address and the role the company will play in the order.

A supplier may be a manufacturer, a converter, an exporter or a trading company. Any of these roles can work, but the responsibility must be clear. If a trading company supplies the material, ask who makes it and whose TDS, COA and complaint records will support the order.

Check that these names remain consistent:

- Quotation and sales contract
- Proforma invoice and commercial invoice
- Bank beneficiary
- TDS, SDS and COA issuer
- Packaging and shipping marks
- Company address and contact details

Ask for the scope of any management-system certificate. An ISO 9001 certificate, for example, supports a quality-management claim for the activities and sites named in the certificate. It does not establish that a particular masterbatch grade meets the buyer's color, processing or regulatory requirement.

A live video review or site visit can help confirm that the reported address, equipment and material handling match the company's description. Where travel is not practical, ask for a short live walk-through rather than relying only on edited photographs.

## Read the documents by scope

The value of a technical document comes from the product, grade, batch or test it covers. A document with no clear scope cannot support a broad supplier claim.

| Document | What it can help confirm | Questions to ask |
|---|---|---|
| TDS | Product identification, stated properties, test methods, conditions and intended scope | Which grade and version is this? Is each value typical, specified or an acceptance limit? What test conditions apply? |
| COA | Results for a specific production batch | Which batch is covered? Who tested it? Which methods were used? Is this an example or the record for the material being shipped? |
| SDS | Safety information for handling, storage and transport | Which product and version does it cover? Which language or jurisdiction is it prepared for? |
| Compliance report or declaration | A specific test or regulatory claim tied to a defined scope | Which grade or sample was tested? Which standard, laboratory, date and result apply? Does the scope match the intended market and end use? |
| Management-system certificate | Certification of defined activities, sites or processes | Is the certificate current? What is the certified scope? Does it cover the site and process used for this order? |

Three distinctions prevent most document mistakes.

First, a TDS is not a batch certificate. A typical value describes the product in general terms. A specification states a requirement the supplier expects to meet. An acceptance value belongs to an agreement between buyer and supplier. Ask which type of value appears in the document.

Second, a COA covers one batch unless the document clearly says otherwise. Ask for the COA or inspection record tied to the material being offered, not an undated example passed around as a quality brochure.

Third, a compliance document is limited to the grade, sample, standard and test shown in it. A RoHS report for one black sample does not cover every black grade. An FDA-related report for one white sample does not make the full white range suitable for food contact. Use the [technical document request](/technical-documents/) process when the exact grade and intended use still need review.

## Test whether the numbers are usable

A number without a method and condition can create false confidence. The same property measured under different conditions may not be directly comparable.

For melt flow rate or melt volume flow rate, ask whether the result is MFR or MVR, the temperature and load used, the unit, and whether the document reports a typical value or a specification. A melt-flow result supports quality control. It does not prove how the material will run on a particular machine.

For color difference, the numeric result depends on the reference sample, base resin, masterbatch addition ratio, sample preparation, instrument, illuminant and measurement conditions. A delta E value without those details is not enough for supplier comparison.

For pigment or filler content, ask what the percentage refers to and whether it is measured, calculated or stated as a nominal formulation value. Carbon black loading, TiO2 content and CaCO3 filler can change blackness, opacity, gloss, density and cost. They should be compared only with the grade and test basis in view.

## Judge how the supplier handles your application

A supplier that understands masterbatch should ask questions before recommending a grade. Useful questions include:

- What resin is the product made from?
- Is the process blown film, injection molding, extrusion, blow molding, spinning or another method?
- What is the final product and which property matters most?
- What is the current appearance or performance target?
- Is there an existing grade, sample, color reference or production problem?
- What addition ratio is planned or currently used?
- What documents are required for the target market and end use?

A supplier may not need every answer before the first reply. The useful signal is whether the supplier recognizes the missing information and explains what must be confirmed next. A recommendation that ignores resin, process and end use has little technical value.

Look for answers that separate facts from assumptions. A careful reply may say:

> The TDS lists this grade for PE film. We still need the exact resin, film thickness and target opacity before confirming the trial direction.

That kind of response is easier to verify than "suitable for all films".

## Compare cost with the full product basis

Two quotations are comparable only when they describe the same product and commercial basis. Before ranking price, confirm:

- Grade and product version
- Carrier resin
- Pigment, filler or functional content where relevant
- Packaging size and material
- Quantity basis
- Incoterm and destination
- Currency
- Quote validity
- Expected lead time
- Payment terms
- Whether the price includes the required documents or tests

A cheaper price per kilogram may require a higher addition ratio. It may also include more filler, a different carrier, a smaller package or a less convenient delivery term. Compare expected cost against the finished product using the supplier's stated basis, then confirm that basis in a trial.

## Review the sample and trial plan

Ask for a sample that carries a grade name, batch or date reference and handling information. A sample with no identity is difficult to compare with the production material later.

Before the trial, agree on:

- Exact masterbatch grade and sample reference
- Base resin and grade
- Addition ratio and feeding method
- Machine and main processing conditions
- Product or film structure, thickness or wall section
- Control sample for comparison
- Properties to inspect
- Pass or follow-up criteria
- Who will record the result

A trial can show that one identified sample ran in a defined setup. It does not establish batch-to-batch consistency by itself. Ask how the next production batch will be checked and which records will be supplied with it.

The [Buyer FAQ](/faq/) covers related questions about trial samples and document review.

## Check batch control and complaint handling

An acceptable first sample is useful. A supplier also needs a repeatable way to tell the buyer when something changes.

Ask how the supplier:

- Identifies production batches
- Keeps retained samples
- Records inspection results
- Handles a change in raw material, formulation or process
- Notifies customers when a change may affect the product
- Investigates color, dispersion, contamination or processing complaints

When a quality issue appears, a useful supplier asks for the problem sample, control sample, batch references, resin, addition ratio, processing records and a clear description of the defect before assigning a cause. "Not our problem" is not an investigation.

The supplier should also explain what its COA covers. A COA that reports only one property may be valid for that property and insufficient for another requirement. Ask for the methods and results that match the decision you need to make.

## Put the commercial terms under change control

Product and commercial assumptions change together. A new resin, target color, packaging format, destination or monthly quantity can alter the grade, document requirement or quotation.

Create a short change record whenever one of these items changes:

| Changed item | Previous record | New record | Effect on sample, documents or quotation | Next action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
|  |  |  |  |  |

The record does not need to be complicated. It needs to prevent an old sample, COA or quotation from being treated as valid after the conditions have changed.

## A 45-minute desk review

Use this sequence before moving a supplier to the sample stage.

### Minutes 0 to 10: identity

Confirm the legal entity, address, role in the supply chain and consistency between quotation, invoice and bank details. Ask who issues the TDS and COA.

### Minutes 10 to 20: product definition

Check that the supplier can identify the exact grade, carrier, intended process and main product properties. Mark any claim that uses only vague category language.

### Minutes 20 to 30: document scope

Request the TDS and a COA example for the offered grade. For any compliance claim, ask for the grade, sample, standard, test date and intended scope. Make a list of what is still missing.

### Minutes 30 to 40: technical response

Send the same application brief used for other suppliers. Compare whether the reply addresses resin, process, end use, target and documents, or jumps directly to a price.

### Minutes 40 to 45: next step

Decide whether the supplier should receive a sample request, provide more documents or remain on hold. Record the reason. Do not move forward only because the sales response was fast.

## Warning signs

Treat these as questions for further review:

- The quotation, invoice, bank beneficiary and document issuer use unrelated company names.
- The supplier sends a TDS for a different grade.
- The supplier says all products meet every certification without giving a grade or test scope.
- A COA has no batch reference or test method.
- A technical value has no unit, condition or indication of whether it is typical or specified.
- The supplier promises suitability without asking about resin, process or end use.
- The supplier cannot explain how a batch change is communicated.
- A complaint is answered with blame before any evidence is collected.
- The quoted material cannot be tied to a sample or production batch reference.

None of these points automatically ends a supplier review. Each one creates a follow-up question and a record of what remains unverified.

## A short supplier review email

This message can be sent after the first product discussion. Adjust the fields to the actual application.

> Subject: Technical and document review for [product / grade]
>
> Hello [name],
>
> Before we compare the quotation, please confirm the following:
>
> 1. Legal entity name, production site and your role in the supply chain
> 2. Exact grade and carrier resin being quoted
> 3. TDS version and date
> 4. Which TDS values are typical and which are specifications
> 5. COA example for the offered grade, with batch reference and test methods
> 6. SDS version for the product being supplied
> 7. Any compliance document, including the grade or sample, standard, test date and scope
> 8. Recommended sample quantity and trial conditions for [resin, process and application]

> 10. How you notify customers about changes that may affect product performance

> Our application brief is attached. Please identify any information we still need to provide.
>
> Regards,
> [buyer]

## What a good answer looks like

A good supplier answer is specific enough to check. It names the grade, document, batch or test being discussed. It states assumptions. It says when a question needs the buyer's resin, process or end use before an answer can be given.

That standard applies to every supplier. Product pages can help shortlist a material direction, but they do not replace the supplier review.
