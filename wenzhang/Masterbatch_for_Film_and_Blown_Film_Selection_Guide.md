Blown film makes some material problems easy to see. A particle, streak or color variation that stays hidden inside a thick molded part can appear across a thin film within seconds. That visibility helps quality control, but it also makes film masterbatch selection less forgiving.

The useful question is not simply "which grade is for film?" Film is a process and a family of products. A shopping bag, agricultural film, lamination layer and co-extruded barrier structure may all be blown, yet they can place different demands on resin, carrier, dispersion, opacity and documents.

This guide sets out the information a buyer should gather before asking for a grade, the questions to put to a supplier, and the trial conditions that make a result comparable.

## Define the film before choosing the masterbatch

A supplier needs five points before it can review a film masterbatch direction:

- Base resin and grade
- Monolayer or co-extruded structure
- Layer where the masterbatch will be used
- Film thickness and expected let-down ratio
- Final application and required documents

Record the actual resin grade where possible. PE, LDPE, LLDPE and PP are useful families, but they do not describe every polymer grade in the plant. Two PE resins can differ in melt behavior, density and processing window. The masterbatch carrier has to be considered against the resin actually used.

For a co-extruded film, state which layer carries the masterbatch. A skin layer, core layer and tie layer serve different functions. A product that works in one layer or structure is not automatically suitable for another.

Film thickness affects how visible a defect becomes and how much color or opacity must be delivered from the added masterbatch. The application determines whether the priority is blackness, whiteness, color, barrier support, surface behavior or another function. It also determines which technical documents may be needed.

The [masterbatch RFQ checklist](/resources/masterbatch-rfq-checklist/) can be used to collect these inputs in one format.

## Check carrier compatibility

Masterbatch is a concentrate carried into the melt by a carrier resin and other components. The carrier must melt and mix within the process window of the base resin. A PE carrier is a starting direction for a PE film, not proof that every PE grade has been checked.

Ask the supplier:

- What carrier does the grade use?
- Has the carrier been reviewed for this resin family and film process?
- Does the supplier expect any difference in mixing, feeding or surface behavior?
- If the resin changes, which part of the previous trial remains relevant?

The last question matters during sourcing changes. A trial result belongs to the identified resin, masterbatch, machine and conditions. If one of those changes, the buyer should review whether a new trial or supplier confirmation is needed.

## Select black, white and color by different criteria

Black, white and color masterbatch all pass through the same film line, but they do not raise the same technical questions.

### Black masterbatch

Black film is often judged by blackness and consistency, yet dispersion can matter more than the headline carbon black percentage. Carbon black is a fine pigment, and poorly dispersed agglomerates can appear as specks or streaks in thin film.

Ask for the carrier resin, carbon black loading, intended addition range and dispersion controls. Request the current TDS for the exact grade. Where a trial is proposed, define the target blackness, film thickness and addition ratio before running it.

A high carbon black percentage may support a lower addition ratio, but the buyer should compare cost in use and dispersion rather than the percentage alone. The [black masterbatch guide](/products/black-masterbatch/) explains the relationship between loading, filler and grade position.

### White masterbatch

White film depends on the balance between whiteness, opacity, TiO2 content, filler, carrier and film thickness. A whiteness statement without a reference, addition ratio and resin condition is difficult to compare.

Ask:

- What whiteness or opacity target was used?
- Is the value typical, specified or an acceptance value?
- In which resin, at which addition ratio and film thickness was it assessed?
- What is the TiO2 content, and what else contributes to opacity?
- Which document covers a required food-contact or other regulated use?

A document should identify the grade or sample, test method, date and scope. "Food-grade white masterbatch" is a marketing label unless the required document supports the exact product and intended use. The [white masterbatch guide](/products/white-masterbatch/) keeps those questions separate from the purchase decision.

### Color masterbatch

Color selection begins with a reference. A physical sample, approved color standard, Pantone or RAL reference, existing product or previous supplier grade gives the supplier something to assess. A photograph can help communication, but it is usually a weak color reference because lighting, camera settings and screens change the appearance.

Ask how the supplier will judge the match. The answer should include the resin, addition ratio, sample preparation, measurement method and acceptable difference where a numeric target is used. The buyer should also confirm whether the reference is the final acceptance standard.

Color matching is a process that continues through sampling and production. The [color masterbatch guide](/products/color-masterbatch/) explains the inputs needed for an RFQ.

## Review the let-down ratio as a system variable

The let-down ratio is the amount of masterbatch added to the base resin. It influences color, opacity, dispersion demand and cost. It also changes how much carrier enters the final formulation.

For each proposed grade, ask:

- What addition ratio did the supplier use to develop or describe the product?
- Is that a starting recommendation, a typical production range or a customer-specific value?
- What feeding method was used?
- Did the supplier assess the final film at the planned ratio?

Compare cost in use where possible. Calculate the cost of masterbatch required for a defined production quantity, then check whether the trial result was obtained at that ratio. A lower price per kilogram with a higher use rate may not reduce cost.

The current TDS is the starting document for grade-specific values. Any addition range should remain tied to the exact product and should be confirmed in the trial.

## Check processing conditions and line response

Blown film trials can expose problems that a datasheet does not describe: unstable bubbles, die build-up, gels, specks, surface defects, excessive pressure or inconsistent color. Ask the supplier which processing conditions matter for the grade and how those conditions were assessed.

Record the actual line conditions used in the trial:

- Extruder zones and melt temperature
- Screw speed and output
- Die and air conditions
- Frost line and film thickness
- Screen pack or filtration setup
- Feeding method and addition ratio
- Start-up, purge and changeover steps

A supplier does not need to guarantee every machine setting. It should be able to explain which conditions affect the product and which observations would require follow-up.

## Use a trial with agreed conditions

A sample trial is more useful when both parties agree on what will be compared. Before the material arrives, record:

| Trial input | What to record |
|---|---|
| Masterbatch | Grade, sample reference, batch or date where available |
| Base resin | Resin type, grade and batch |
| Film structure | Monolayer or co-extruded, layer position and thickness |
| Addition ratio | Planned ratio and feeding method |
| Equipment | Line, screw, die, filter and relevant settings |
| Control | The current film or another agreed comparison sample |
| Test scope | Color, opacity, blackness, dispersion, surface, pressure, bubble stability or other checks |
| Decision rule | What result leads to approval, another trial or rejection |
| Record owner | Who will capture samples and machine data |

A successful trial supports one result in one defined setup. It does not automatically prove every future batch, another line or another film structure. Ask separately how the supplier controls batch consistency and what records accompany production.

## Read the TDS with the film application in view

A TDS can identify the product, carrier, typical properties, test methods and intended scope. It does not replace a film trial.

Check the document version and grade first. Then read each value with its method and condition. Confirm whether the number is typical, specified or an acceptance value. For color, opacity, melt flow, moisture and dispersion-related properties, the method and conditions determine whether the values are comparable.

If the film requires a technical or compliance document, ask for the exact scope. A TDS, SDS, COA, test report and declaration answer different questions. Use the [technical document request](/technical-documents/) process when document availability and applicability still need review.

## Questions to send a supplier

This set is short enough for a first technical exchange:

1. Which grade do you recommend for this resin, film structure, thickness and application?
2. What carrier does the grade use?
3. What is the intended addition ratio, and what basis supports it?
4. Which properties should be checked before the trial?
5. Which current TDS applies to the exact grade?
6. Can you provide a sample with a grade and batch or date reference?
7. What trial conditions should we agree before running the sample?
8. Which documents apply to the target market and end use?
9. How do you control and report batch consistency?
10. What changes would require a new sample review?

When a supplier answers with assumptions and missing inputs rather than a universal promise, that is useful information. It shows where the review must continue.

## Continue with the right technical question

Film selection becomes easier when the buyer separates product category, application and evidence. Start with the masterbatch direction that matches the material and film requirement, then use the exact grade, current documents and a controlled trial to decide whether it belongs in production.
